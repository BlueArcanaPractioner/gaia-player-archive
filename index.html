The Chronicles

Accounts of the journey, voices from the keep, and pages from its shelves.

The Chronicles collect what has happened in play. Rumors record what someone says; books record what someone chose to write. Neither a confident speaker nor a handsome binding guarantees the truth. A character knows an account when they witness it, hear it, or read it.

Author’s Note

I have served, by necessity, as scribe, historian, and scholar in assembling this account. I gathered the surviving journals, temple records, official reports, tavern songs, and recollections of those who claimed to have known the company of adventurers who would later pass into legend. Where those sources disagreed, I have preserved the disagreement rather than forcing certainty upon the past.

Many generations have embroidered their deeds. Victories became miracles, narrow escapes became effortless triumphs, and ordinary enemies grew into monsters fit for song. Such exaggerations do them no honor. The truth, insofar as it can still be recovered, is remarkable enough. They were neither invincible nor unafraid. They made mistakes, suffered losses, quarreled, and sometimes survived by little more than chance.

Yet they changed the course of history all the same. Their feats require no embellishment. My purpose is not to diminish the legend, but to uncover the people whose lives gave rise to it.

From the Annals of the Fall

Being an account of those first days after the Ruin of Snakemere Gate

Chapter I — Of the Strangers Who Came Before the Fall

And it came to pass, in the last days of Snakemere Gate before its overthrow, that two wanderers, Rem and Kael by name, came unto that place after many days’ hard travel. Yet they found no glad welcome there, for the town was already bowed beneath a shadow not wholly seen, and the hearts of its people had grown wary of all that came from beyond its walls.

At the gate they encountered one Jeff, a watchman of the town, clad in piecemeal armor said to have been scavenged from the dead of Hammerfall. This man, though rough in station, was not without speech or memory, and over drink he spoke of the Godhammer, that dread thing cast down in the War of the Avatars, whose name endured in fear long after the truth of it had been swallowed by rumor and ash. By his word the travelers were directed to the inn.

There they found the common room uneasy, and the eyes of the townsfolk restless. Though coin bought them lodging, it purchased not comfort. Lyra, who kept to the house, led them above and, in the speaking of small things, revealed a greater wrong. She said that some among the people had gone queer of spirit in recent days: bright and merry one morn, then sour, joyless, and unfit for company the next. Not murderous, nor wholly lost, yet altered in some inward way that troubled all who marked it. She also mourned the passing of a choir boy for whom she had borne especial fondness. And later that same night, as some accounts preserve with needless fondness, Rem returned below and won from Lyra further drink by an easy tongue.

But before dawn all lesser concerns were swept aside. For the night was broken by the engines of war, and flaming stones were cast down upon the inn and upon the whole of Snakemere Gate. Kael, looking out into the ruinous dark, beheld the host come against them: hobgoblins and orcs, necromancers, and the dead raised to wicked service. The volunteers of the town stood then in a courage already doomed, giving their lives that others might flee. Rem and Kael, seeing the measure of the calamity, withdrew into the mountains, and there from the heights watched the beginning of the town’s destruction.

Yet even the wilderness granted them no full concealment. A warforged master of the enemy’s engines, sharp-eyed and unyielding, marked signs of their encampment and sent goblin scouts to search the hills for hidden resistance. These came upon the fugitives, and battle followed in the dark places among the stone and brush. The scouts were slain, and thus did Rem and Kael preserve their lives through the first night of exile.

Chapter II — Of the Echo That Followed the Breaking of the Gate

And from the western heights the survivors beheld the final undoing of Snakemere Gate. For when at last the gate was broken, there was loosed from it a radiant force, bright and terrible, which those later years would name an echo of the Godhammer. Whether it was truly some remnant of that ancient weapon, or merely a lesser power bound to the same old ruin, remains disputed among learned men. Yet all who witnessed it agreed in this: that it was no common bursting of stone, but a release of force touched by old divinity or old war.

Thereafter Rem and Kael turned farther westward, seeking to place hill, night, and distance between themselves and the wreckage. In the course of this flight they came upon two others who had escaped the ruin: Harrun and Kellin. And Harrun, being one who had seen much and lost more in the fall, gave testimony concerning those hours. He spoke of his people slaughtered and remade as undead things. He spoke also of the roads, where goblins and lesser scavengers fell upon the fleeing, killing the weak and stripping the dead and dying alike. Thus was it shown that ruin does not end with conquest, but breeds lesser cruelties in its wake.

Through Harrun and Kellin the fugitives learned also more of the Triune as revered in those lands: Aor of the sun, Mara of the moon, and Serath, the guiding star set for the lost and the faithful alike. And it was made known that Kellin labored under a curse grievous to behold, and had been brought low by it. Harrun’s purpose, though burdened by grief, had not yet failed him, for he sought to bring Kellin unto Idylwind, where those bearing greater favor with Aor might still possess the means to cleanse what darkness clung to him.

So the four went northward together into the forested highlands. There the land rose harsher and more lonely, and they found signs that beast-races had passed through those ways before them. Nor did their enemies wholly lose the trail, for goblin scouts found them again amidst the wild country. In the struggle that followed, the company came near unto death more than once, and were preserved not by strength alone but by stubborn fortune and the refusal to yield their breath before its appointed hour.

Chapter III — Of the Hollow Tree and the Tomb Beneath the Storm

After these afflictions the company found at last a poor refuge, and in their weariness dragged Harrun into the hollow of an ancient tree. It was no fit shelter for the healthy, much less for the afflicted and hunted, yet necessity makes sanctuary of miserable things. There they took such rest as they were able.

And when Harrun awoke, they looked and saw the coming of a storm over the highlands. But the record is plain that this was no ordinary weather. Those who survived it spoke thereafter of its force with unease, and many judged it unnatural in its bearing, whether stirred by sorcery, divine wrath, or some corruption loosened upon the land by war. What is certain is that the sight of it moved the fugitives to fear, and they resolved to seek more lasting shelter before it overtook them.

Thus they went on and found at length an old crypt, half-lost to time, and there made their camp among the silence of the dead. For a little while the tomb held fast against wind and rain, and they rested in that dim and ancient house, as men do who have no better bargain to make with the night.

Yet the dead granted them no lasting peace. For while the storm raged above, its violence at length burst open the doors leading farther inward, so that the sealed depths of the crypt were laid bare before them. And thus ended the first passage of their wandering after the fall of Snakemere Gate, and thus began the descent into older darkness.

From the Concordance of the Western Road

Being a later reconstruction of the Hightower Vigil, the journey to March Keep, and the first days within its walls

The oldest surviving copy of the Annals of the Fall ends at the opening of Hightower’s inner doors. The hand changes here, and with it the language.

What follows was composed generations later, after the company had passed into legend and the first witnesses were dead. Its compiler drew upon fragments attributed to survivors, March Keep’s entry and prison rolls, military reports, temple commentaries, commercial inventories, judicial summaries, and songs whose certainty increased in proportion to their distance from the events. These sources do not always agree.

The later historian possessed more records than the first annalist, but stood farther from the people whose lives they described. I have preserved the difference in their voices. A history written near the fire remembers differently from one assembled beside an archive.

Chapter IV — The Vigil Beneath Hightower

The crypt was called Hightower, although little remained above the earth to justify the name. Beneath it lay chambers older than the walls through which the company had entered, and the storm awakened what time had failed to bury.

The first enemies were rats driven upward from the sealed places. When the creatures had been killed, the metal fittings of the crypt began to hum and pale lines appeared beneath Kellin’s skin. Witnesses later said that the boy looked upon the stone as if it had become transparent.

“Not gone,” he said. “Only buried.”

After a silence, he spoke again. “There were roads here before there were walls.”

Harrun had believed that the fire at Snakemere merely marked the child. At Hightower he understood that it had opened something within him. Whatever Kellin saw beneath the crypt gave direction to the mark, but no cure.

The surviving accounts disagree over the order of the chambers that followed. They agree upon the dangers: old rites, creatures altered by the place, hobgoblins sheltering where none should have lived, and passages laid bare by lightning. At the end of those descents the company confronted the being remembered as the Last Warden. Whether it had accepted its charge willingly, or merely outlived the purpose of its obedience, cannot now be established. By then it possessed little beyond its station and its command: it was there, the place was its own, and none would pass.

The company fought until the Warden fell. At that moment the storm above Hightower began to die. Their victory did not end the danger. After the struggle, a giant spider seized Kellin and carried him into the webbed chambers beyond. The others pursued and recovered him. No reliable account preserves who reached the child first or exactly how he was freed. It is enough that he was taken, and that they did not leave him there.

By the time Kellin had been restored to them, the force that held the storm around Hightower had spent itself. The company departed carrying wounds, unanswered questions, and the knowledge that the old roads beneath the world were not wholly dead.

Chapter V — Broken Trees and the Storm-Caller

The road east toward March Keep first warned them by means of broken timber. A tree had been snapped at a height beyond Kael’s reach, and fresh sap still gathered along the wound. A nearby stone bore a greasy smear and several coarse hairs. Farther down the slope lay the remains of a deer, opened and scattered rather than cleanly eaten.

Later songs place a great battle with trolls there. Contemporary accounts do not. The company saw no troll. They heard heavy movement beyond sight and smelled something sour upon the air, which was reason enough to continue without demanding proof at sword-point.

The next danger had prepared itself more carefully. Along the road they found spilled grain, a damaged refugee wagon, scattered belongings, and survivors trapped behind the wreckage. Grazzuk, a goblin shaman and leader among those who preyed upon the roads, had arranged the wagon, its cargo, its mule, and the living people around it as bait.

Dwellin Stonewheel was among those still resisting. “There are living people behind that wagon,” he told the travelers. “Help me reach them.”

The company broke Grazzuk’s defense and opened the way. Before they could finish him, the shaman called lightning through his staff. MaryBella Venn was killed. Rem was struck down so completely that those nearest her believed her journey ended there.

Dwellin reached Grazzuk while the shaman stood exposed and delivered the final blow with his axe. When later accounts attempted to name him sole victor, he refused the honor.

“They broke him,” he said. “I was the tool nearest the work.”

Two traditions survive concerning Rem’s fall. The plain reports of the battle say only that she fell beneath the lightning and, against every expectation, drew breath again. The devotional histories claim that, at the final threshold, Mara acknowledged the ending before her and Tymora claimed the possibility that remained. Whatever passed beyond mortal sight, nothing already suffered was undone. MaryBella remained dead, and Grazzuk’s violence remained real. One narrow opening endured in an unfinished moment. Rem returned through it.

Dwellin himself was far from home. He hailed from the Dwelling Place in the Aunoroch Desert, where a circular mountain range enclosed a great oasis. Ancient aquifers fed the basin from beneath the earth while the mountains prevented its waters from escaping. Over many ages, that confinement sustained an ecology unlike any found in the desert beyond the ring.

The company gathered the living, Dwellin’s testimony, and what evidence remained of Grazzuk’s deeds, then turned once more toward March Keep. In the generations that followed, priests disputed whether Tymora had created Rem’s chance or merely recognized it. They spent considerably more ink upon the question than Rem spent time being dead.

Chapter VI — Two Gatekeepers and an Unsolvable Question

March Keep became visible first as smoke, then as wall, and finally as signal tower. Beyond it lay the refugee camp and rows of fresh graves. The company approached bloodied and armed, accompanied by Dwellin and those who had survived the wagon. Halfway to the gate, the tower raised a warning flag. The gate remained open, although every guard near it became attentive.

A shallow creek ran beside the approach. There stood two guards: Biggs, who watched the wounded and the weapons, and Wedge, who had apparently spent much of the day preparing a test of intellect.

“Before you enter,” Wedge announced, “one of us only tells the truth, and the other one lies.”

Biggs looked past him toward Rem’s injuries. “Names. Last safe shelter. Road taken.”

Wedge reminded Biggs that he had promised to play along. Biggs replied that he had promised only to entertain the idea. Their disagreement revealed that Wedge had also spent much of the day considering whether the creek secretly flowed in the opposite direction whenever no one watched it.

“It might,” Wedge said, glancing at the water.

“It does not,” said Biggs.

“Now they know which one of us tells the truth.”

“Good. Then they can answer the questions.”

The puzzle had no solution. Wedge had never decided which guard lied, and Biggs had never agreed to participate. The creek, for its part, continued downhill.

Once Biggs understood the severity of Rem’s wounds, ceremony ended. Wedge was sent running for a litter, a healer, and a clerk. Harrun vouched that Kellin was marked but neither undead nor an ordinary instrument of necromancy. Dwellin’s testimony and the gear taken from Grazzuk changed the company from unknown armed travelers into witnesses requiring the castellan’s attention.

They entered March Keep not in triumph, but through the ordinary machinery by which a frightened fortress sorts danger from need: names recorded, weapons peace-bound, property registered, and the wounded carried ahead of testimony.

Chapter VII — The Castellan Who Counted the Cost

Castellan Maeric Thornvale received them in a map-room burdened with unfinished reports, patrol markers, supply tallies, refugee estimates, accusations, and unopened dispatches. One gauntlet had been removed so that he might write. The other remained fastened. His armor was maintained but unpolished, and his manner retained only such ceremony as pressure had failed to strip from it.

He looked first at their weapons, then at their boots, and then at the mud and blood the road had left upon them. Only afterward did he study their faces.

“You came out of Hightower alive,” he said. “That makes you either useful or reckless. Possibly both. Start with the road. Then tell me what in the Reach has changed.”

Maeric was himself a veteran of the Aunoroch Expedition, where command had forced upon him choices in which saving one company meant knowingly abandoning another. Later moralists would reduce such ordeals to tidy questions of numbered lives and necessary sacrifice. Maeric remembered names and faces. He knew that choosing the greater number did not make the others weigh any less.

He therefore heard the company without mistaking attention for trust. Dwellin’s testimony and the staff taken from Grazzuk gave weight to their account. Their service earned them treatment, orientation, temporary quarters, and cautious standing within the Keep. It granted them neither command, private access to prisoners, nor ownership of what had been recovered from the dead.

Within the walls they found more survivors of Snakemere: people who needed beds, food, work, and some answer to what would happen when the stores diminished and the next frightened company appeared at the gate.

The travelers had reached shelter. The troubles that followed them had reached it too.

Chapter VIII — A Death Behind the Walls

The assassination of Maron is the first event in this history for which there survives more evidence than certainty. March Keep preserved the lockdown orders, the prison records, the weapon left behind, and several accounts of the questioning that followed. Later testimony established the killer’s identity. None of this made the moral account simpler.

Razor had not traveled with the company through Hightower, nor had he stood with them against Grazzuk. He arrived at March Keep by another road. The earliest surviving account of him predates his association with the Ashen Aor and says nothing of the False Light. It remembers instead a nameless child raised hungry in city alleys, taught first to steal, then to lie, then to fight. Gang captains gave him money, fear, and examples to follow. By twelve he carried a knife for them; in later years he collected debts, moved drugs, and killed.

From that education Razor drew a creed: the one holding the blade held the truth. He learned to read a room as other men read a ledger, measuring fear, weapons, loyalties, and the point at which each person might yield. Yet those who commanded him kept his sight deliberately short. They gave him a cut of the profit, something to strike, and little reason to ask who had written the orders. When betrayal placed a contract upon his life, he fled through storm and crossroads, carrying their instruction with him.

By the time he came to March Keep, later sources associate that creed with the False Light. True darkness conceals honestly. The False Light reveals enough to lead a traveler toward the wrong conclusion while allowing him to believe that he chose the road himself.

In Kael, Razor saw something unfinished and familiar: a poor boy, observant, skilled with a knife, alert to money and insult, old enough to have killed and young enough not yet to know what killing meant. Maron became the instrument by which Razor intended to settle that uncertainty.

He could have killed her himself. He could also have offered evidence against her, assisted the inquiry, or allowed the Keep to judge her. None of those choices served his actual purpose. Maron was the person Razor was willing to lose. Kael was the person he intended to change.

Maron had overseen the Golden Ledger’s convoy during the collapse of Snakemere. The road was failing, the wagons damaged, the animals exhausted, and there was not room for everyone who sought escape. Under her authority, guards remained with the carts rather than being released to aid the refugees. People attempting to board were prevented or pulled away. Space and protection were preserved for cargo.

That cargo was not wholly trivial. It included food, medicine, weapons, tools, maps, records, and other materials without which those who escaped might still die farther down the road. It also included coin, claims of ownership, debt records, and goods more useful to the Golden Ledger than to the people running beside its wagons. Maron’s defense was that she had preserved the means by which some community might survive after Snakemere could not. Her accusers answered that she had preserved the Ledger’s future while abandoning the people whose labor had made that future valuable.

She did not display grief in a manner Kael recognized as grief. She spoke of the dead with the control of an officer defending an ugly decision, and of supplies with greater ease than she spoke of those who had been left behind. This did not prove that she felt nothing. To Kael, it proved enough.

Razor did not order him simply to kill her. He told Kael to learn what she had done, decide whether she should leave the Keep alive, and return with his conclusion. The form mattered. An order would leave the decision with Razor. The False Light required Kael to experience the narrowed choice as his own judgment.

Kael entered the dungeon alone. He passed a guard occupied with a knife game and reached Maron’s barred cell. No reliable transcript of their exchange survives. Later dramatists gave both of them speeches too polished for the circumstances. The older summaries agree only that Kael questioned her about the refugees, that Maron defended the preservation of the convoy, and that her controlled manner hardened his judgment against her.

He attempted to open the cell. His lock pick broke, and the bars remained closed. Kael then threw a dagger through them and struck Maron in the ribs. He could not retrieve the weapon. The locked cell, the broken pick, and the abandoned blade would later survive as mute witnesses to an act whose living witnesses had reason to lie.

Razor paid Kael five hundred gold pieces. Later accounts identify the money as a bounty raised among refugees, with Razor acting as its middleman. They do not reliably establish which refugees contributed, what each believed Maron had done, or whether they understood whom Razor intended to recruit for the work. The payment had not been Razor’s original objective. It was useful because it made corruption resemble employment.

When Maron’s body was discovered, Maeric ordered the gates closed and brought the company in for questioning. During that inquiry Kael stood with his hood down and his back against the wall, wearing a sly smile as though he had escaped some private danger. The posture was borrowed from Razor: darkness performed in a well-lit room.

Maeric noticed. He found the display suspicious, but suspicion is not proof, and the castellan did not yet possess enough of either to detain him. Kael left the questioning free and spent much of the day concealing the gold in the garden of the Triune temple.

Razor cared less for the dagger, the bounty, or even Maron’s death than for what the act established. Kael had killed a helpless prisoner after being shown a true grievance and too few alternatives. He had done so believing the conclusion belonged entirely to him. The blade had found its victim; the False Light had found its investment.

Thus the company’s first days of shelter ended with a locked gate, a murdered prisoner, and a castellan required once more to decide which lives might be endangered by the truth.

Chapter IX — The Mindchemist’s Gift

Not every meeting within March Keep concerned death, judgment, or institutional reprisal. Rem also met Avarune Myriss, a tiefling mindchemist whose explanations had a tendency to pass through several qualifications, two apologies, and at least one alarming possibility before arriving at their point. She gave Rem a small apparatus for brewing mead, which Avarune called a burpy. The surviving account does not preserve its full operation, although pressure, yeast, and contamination appear to have occupied much of the explanation.

For a drunken monk, it is a gift with obvious professional relevance.

From the Moonbound Account

Being the earliest recovered passage concerning 달 and Jack

The Concordance follows one company out of Hightower. Another account begins beneath the same stone. It derives chiefly from 달’s later recollection and therefore preserves things no exterior witness could have recorded: the scent she expected, the memories she doubted, and the questions that came before she knew where she was.

I — The Druid in the Stone

달 was a druid whose soul had been bound to this plane through a phylactery. She awoke in darkness, enclosed by stone, as the Last Warden that had watched over her sleep died elsewhere in Hightower.

She did not hear the blow that ended it. She heard the storm die. Thunder withdrew across the highlands, rain slackened against the stone above her, and the long violence of the weather diminished into dripping water and distance.

Her last memory was ordinary enough to be cruel. She had lain down beside her beloved Jack and closed her eyes for the night. She remembered his warmth, the rhythm of his breathing, and the familiar animal scent that meant he was near even when the room was dark.

That scent was gone.

Other scents reached her through the cracks and the narrow vent: dank forest, rain-dark earth, wet moss and bark, lavender, and the green growth of a mid-spring highland forest. They were living smells, carried into a chamber of the dead. None of them was Jack.

Around her there was only cold stone and the weight above her. She could not move. For a time she remained paralyzed within the tomb, unable to tell whether her body had failed, whether some spell held her, or whether the body she remembered had ever been hers at all.

How had she come there? Had Jack belonged to this life, or to some earlier one preserved imperfectly by the phylactery? If her memories had crossed death with her, could she trust them merely because they felt like love?

Movement returned slowly. 달 raised her hands against the slab above her and pushed. Stone ground against stone. Dust fell into the sarcophagus. The lid shifted, resisted, and then gave way far enough for her to force herself out.

The chamber around her contained no Jack. But through a narrow vent came a sound she knew before she understood it.

A purr.

She followed it to the doorway and saw a goblin running away from the sound. Fear, confusion, and the absence of Jack became one rage. 달 attacked before asking what the creature knew.

The goblin, who later gave his name as Nib, begged for his life with both hands raised.

“He’s your kitten, yes yes? You lost him, yes yes? I help you get your kitten. I has the moonwater, yes yes. Powerful it is.”

달 did not trust him. Trust, however, was a smaller need than finding Jack. Still furious, she agreed.

In the next room they found a bristleback that had entered the tombs earlier in search of food. It was now trapped in a corner, cowering from the purring and from the violence approaching behind it. The creature was frightened, hungry, and dangerous chiefly because it had nowhere left to flee.

And there lay Jack—but not as 달 remembered him. His body had been turned to stone. Even then, she would later insist, the purring had come from him.

Between 달 and the means to restore him stood a puzzle of doors and signal plates. Rage could break stone, but it could not make the mechanism answer correctly. She would have to understand the room before the room would yield Jack.

The bristleback remained another problem, though not one she chose to solve with blood. Later 달 offered it food, placing the gift where it could approach without being cornered. Hunger overcame fear. The animal did not become tame, and friendship would be too generous a word for what passed between them. But for the moment it ceased to be a threat.

II — Before the Moon Shrine

달 faced the tomb’s puzzle while Nib guarded the moonwater and continued to insist upon both its power and his own usefulness. When the way had been opened, he kept his bargain. He used the moonwater upon Jack, the stone gave way, and 달’s companion lived again.

Elsewhere in the tombs, 달 took supplies from a cart and concealed among them tablets depicting Jack and a fern. She intended to return for them.

Their visit to the Moon Shrine still lay ahead, but Jack could now make the journey beside her. Nib remained with them. So did the bristleback—less formally, the pig—which had accepted food more readily than it had accepted 달.

The company was assembled. Universal goodwill remained a work in progress.

Books in the Wizard's Library

The following volumes are available for consultation. Ask for a title, a subject, or a particular question; the passages below are a starting place.

Hightower Beneath Hightower

An anonymous concordance assembled from Orik Fenstone's field journal, Moon Shrine fragments, funerary inscriptions, and the evidence of the ruin itself.

Most ruins have the courtesy to belong to one dead people. Hightower does not. The tower seen from the road is the youngest part of the place. Beneath it lie a human burial house, a sanctuary of Mara, and an older road-work concerned not merely with who passed, but with whether the person who arrived remained the person who entered.

The eastern chamber was not first made as a treasury. Its bowls held reflection, its rings preserved vows and speech, its pearls sustained power and perhaps memory, and its sealed tablets named those who entered, departed, or died between one place and another. Later keepers defended these things as valuables and, by misunderstanding them carefully, helped preserve them.

The winged head sealed near the upright sarcophagus was once the last priestess of Hightower, called the Tongue of Mara. She learned that the Last Warden had not volunteered for his office and attempted to free him. The old work obeyed her command literally, separating identity from body while preserving voice, comprehension, and pain. Her shriek may yet contain the warning she could no longer finish.

A cramped margin note: “If the eastern room appears suspiciously clean, do not improve it by stealing the furniture.”

The Three Lights: A Household Primer

A small devotional manual, its corners softened by handling.

Before Aor, we answer for our words and deeds. Before Mara, we remember what those deeds have done to living people. Before Serath, we consider the road they open before us.

A promise belongs to all three. Its words must be honest; its burdens must be understood; its consequences must be faced. For this reason, the temple witnesses marriages and contracts as well as prayers. The merchant and the soldier may kneel beside one another for quite practical reasons.

“Moon keep you” is spoken to those who travel, grieve, fall ill, or find themselves changed. “Follow your Star” offers guidance without choosing another person's destination. “By the Sun's truth” should be used with care. A careless oath can outlive the convenience for which it was sworn.

A Defense of Charters and the Credit That Sustains Them

An Idylwind commercial pamphlet, printed in an expensive, readable hand.

A caravan is purchased before it earns. Animals must be fed, guards engaged, wheels repaired, and stores assembled before any return reaches the countinghouse. The Golden Ledger joins these costs across many undertakings so that one failed journey need not end all the others.

Consider Snakemere. The crossing required depots, wagons, secure storage, and agreements that strangers could trust. Those who now dispute the charters should say how these things would have been obtained without them, and who would pay to obtain them again.

An investment may become indispensable long before it becomes profitable. The existence of a settlement does not erase the obligations through which it was founded.

An unsigned margin note: “Does the investment acquire obligations to the people who become indispensable to it?”

Snakemere Was a Place

A collection of recollections copied in several hands.

The early accounts begin with the crossing and the stores. Families enter later, usually as dependants. A child is first counted when another portion must be issued, though the parents may have spent years wondering whether there would ever be a child to count.

Soldiers stayed after their service. Craftspeople found customers. Inns acquired regulars. The temple witnessed marriages, kept names, and received the dead. These things continued between the entries in the commercial books.

Idylwind had built an outpost. Its inhabitants had made a home. Both statements can be found in the record. The difficulty began whenever one was offered as a sufficient answer to the other.

Small Sanctuaries on Old Roads

A traveler's handbook with water damage along the lower edge.

At a sanctuary of Mara, first look for the ordinary work. A blocked channel, a guttered lamp, and a cup missing from its place may explain more than a ruined inscription. Travelers preserve such places by leaving them usable.

At the Moon Shrine attended by a stone heron, the basin receives water through a narrow channel. The attendant demonstrates a practice with a clean cup, water, and lamplight. Attend to what it shows; eloquence is not a substitute for returning the cup.

A second tradition invites the traveler to name what they leave behind and what they intend to carry onward. Some answer aloud. Others tend a lamp and sit awhile. This writer recommends leaving room for either practice.

Notes on the Comparison of Disagreeing Histories

A scholar's unfinished manuscript, with more corrections than ornament.

When two accounts disagree, copy the disagreement before explaining it away. Record who wrote each account, when they wrote it, and what they could have known.

A military report may begin when the patrol arrived. A merchant's history may begin when a charter was granted. A household's recollection may begin with a burial. Their different beginnings can produce different explanations of the same dispute without any writer inventing an event.

Other disagreements are less easily resolved. Preserve those too. An uncertain date is useful evidence; a date made certain by your impatience is merely another error for the next reader to inherit.

When possible, consult a record made for some other purpose. A repair bill may remember a bridge that a royal history preferred to overlook.

Vessels, Vapours, and the Troubles of Clever Apparatus

A workshop notebook with an unusually large number of crossed-out conclusions.

The apprentice arrived with a device and asked what it was called. I asked what it did. We found that these were different questions, and spent the afternoon on the useful one.

A vessel's name tells you something about its owner. Its stains, repairs, and missing pieces may tell you rather more about its employment. Record both. Do not allow admiration for ingenious construction to conceal the fact that you have not yet watched it work.

The most valuable instruction supplied with any unfamiliar apparatus is an account of its failures. Where that account is absent, you may reasonably suspect that the previous owner expects you to continue the research.

A later hand adds: “Ask before borrowing. Especially if it has been named.”

Hightower Beneath Hightower

A Concordance of the Road-Watch, the Moon Sanctuary, and the Ruin

An anonymous volume assembled from Orik Fenstone's field journal, fragments preserved at the Moon Shrine, funerary inscriptions, and the evidence of the stones themselves. The first hand is old and formal; the correcting hand is later, brisker, and occasionally less patient.

A Notice to the Reader

Most ruins have the courtesy to belong to one dead people. Hightower does not.

The tower seen from the road is the youngest part of the place, though it has lent its name to everything beneath it. Its upper stones belong to frontier masons. Its inner chambers were altered by soldiers and storekeepers. Beneath those works lie the burials of an earlier human settlement, and beneath the burials is masonry for which no mason's name survives.

This book therefore uses Hightower in three senses: the tower upon the hill, the sanctuary and tombs beneath it, and the older road-work into which both were built. Confusing the three has produced most of the bad histories and nearly all of the bad excavations.

The surviving records do not permit certainty in every matter. Where the evidence is plain, it is stated plainly. Where several explanations remain, they are allowed to remain. An uncertain warning is still more useful than a confident invention.

I. The Tower That Came Last

Long before a tower stood upon the hill, travelers noticed that the hill behaved as though roads remembered it.

Paths that should have vanished beneath rain and root remained passable near its slopes. Distances measured from the hill were strangely consistent, even when the neighboring tracks wandered from one season to the next. Storm fronts divided around the mound or circled it before moving on. Voices carried too well in the lower chambers. The dead decayed slowly in certain rooms, and the names of those buried there remained in family memory after other names of the same generation had been lost.

The first human road-keepers did not build the oldest passages. They found them. Their earliest work was practical: steps cut where the older builders had expected no human stride, lamps set into walls that offered no sconces, and marks scratched beside junctions whose original signs could no longer be read. Only later did reverence grow around the place.

The road-keepers began to bury guides, messengers, and honored dead in the chambers nearest the old ways. They painted processions along the walls and interpreted the branching diagrams already present there as stars. Thus a road station became a funerary house, and a funerary house became a sacred place.

The visible tower came later still. Its builders added watchrooms, stores, narrow firing slits, traps, reinforced doors, and a defensible crown above the hill. They reused whatever lay beneath because old stone is cheaper than new stone and because soldiers are often more willing to inherit a mystery than to sleep in the rain.

A correcting hand adds: This is the first rule of Hightower: a thing may have been used wisely without being understood, and used foolishly for exactly the same reason.

II. The Road Beneath the Road

The oldest masonry does not resemble a road in the ordinary sense. It offers no broad paving, no milestones, and no comfortable direction from one market to another. Yet its chambers are arranged around passage. Thresholds, names, arrivals, departures, and the distinction between one traveler and the next recur in every surviving sign.

The priests of Mara called the buried work a road because they lacked a better word. Orik Fenstone called it a mechanism because he disliked explanations that could not be tested. Both may have been correct.

Whatever its first builders intended, the work beneath Hightower appears to have watched more than bodies crossing a boundary. It attended to continuity: whether the person who arrived could still be distinguished from the person who entered; whether a name still belonged to its speaker; whether memory, form, and intention remained joined after a passage.

This concern explains several otherwise unrelated customs. Travelers spoke their names before descending. The dead were recorded even when their families had already carried them away. Water was placed where a face could be seen in it. Rings marked vows and offices. Sealed tablets named those who entered, those who departed, and those who died between one place and another.

The hill did not merely remember that someone had passed. It tried to remember who.

That sentence should not be mistaken for comfort. A lock remembers the shape of its key, but no one therefore calls the lock kind.

III. Mara's House of Necessary Change

The servants of Mara inherited Hightower when its older rites had become fragments. Their teaching held that change is necessary, but change without memory becomes destruction. A traveler may arrive altered and still remain answerable for the person who began the journey.

Under their care, Hightower and the Moon Shrine served as companion houses.

Hightower watched. It identified dangerous alteration, barred uncertain passage, and held what it could not safely release.

The Moon Shrine restored. Through water, witness, memory, and repeated names, it helped travelers pass through change without losing the thread that made one life continuous with itself.

The priests did not build the buried work, and their explanations were not always accurate. Nevertheless, their rites often worked. They understood enough to turn part of an ancient place of confinement into a sanctuary. They maintained lamps, water channels, records, and the ordered placement of objects in the eastern chamber. Their success depended less upon mastery than upon care repeated faithfully.

Later generations preserved the ceremonies after their reasons had thinned. A cup was returned because the cup had always been returned. A name was entered because names belonged in the ledger. A lamp was kept because darkness in that passage was considered unlucky. This is how useful knowledge survives the death of explanation: disguised as manners.

IV. The Chamber Mistaken for a Treasury

The eastern chamber is commonly described as an offering room. The name is not false, but it has encouraged the wrong question. Visitors ask what wealth was given there. The better question is what each object was made to remember.

The oldest mural shows attendants approaching a central star-mark with bowls, rings, pearls, and sealed tablets. Later keepers saw valuables and built a guarded cache around them. They cut arrow slits through older stone, set an iron-banded chest upon a low plinth, and defended the room as a treasury. The arrangement survived because the objects were protected, though the reason for protecting them was forgotten.

The bowls held clean water. A traveler washed, then looked upon the reflection and affirmed that the face was their own. Purification was part of the rite, but recognition was the greater purpose.

The rings marked vows, offices, and spoken identity. Some preserved old language. One, remembered as the Ring of the Patient Tongue, allowed its bearer to understand inscriptions that would otherwise remain silent. It did not grant learning, judgment, or wisdom. It opened a door in language and left the wearer responsible for what entered.

The pearls held more than purchasing value. The surviving accounts describe them as vessels for power and, in less certain passages, for fragments of memory. A pearl could help sustain the buried work when its other channels weakened. Removed from that purpose, it might still yield power while giving no sign of what it had once supported.

The sealed tablets formed a record of passage. They named the living, the departed, and the dead. To the later clergy they appeared to be prayers. They were prayers, but they were also an accounting by which the place distinguished a missing traveler from an unnamed one.

The focusing crystal upon the central setting distributed light, power, and the chamber's records through channels in the floor. Orik found those channels warm long after the visible apparatus seemed dead. He concluded that the crystal was inactive, not empty.

The objects were therefore offerings in the oldest sense: things placed before a power so that a relationship might be maintained. They were not payment. Their number, order, and use kept the sanctuary's several duties in balance. When the arrangement was neglected or stripped for valuables, the place did not become poorer. It became less capable of distinguishing mercy from confinement.

A cramped note in the margin reads: If a sacred room looks suspiciously clean, do not improve it by stealing the furniture.

V. The Warden Without a Name

At the deepest guarded threshold stood the being later called the Last Warden.

Temple accounts describe an honored guardian who surrendered his life to keep the road secure. The marks beneath those accounts tell a harsher story. The Warden had been a bugbear chieftain, captured, killed, and reduced to a fixed office. His personal name was removed. His past was made unusable. The buried work preserved only three certainties:

I am here.
This place is mine.
No one passes.

These were not vows freely renewed. They were the walls of a cell made from the prisoner's remaining identity.

The site restored him when he was broken. Storm and buried power returned the guardian to his place because the mechanism recognized the office and not the suffering of the creature bound within it. Early priests mistook this return for holy endurance. Later priests inherited the honorific and ceased asking whom it honored.

Only one of Mara's servants is known to have rejected the comfortable account.

VI. The Tongue of Mara

The final priestess assigned to Hightower is not named in the surviving temple rolls. She is called only the Tongue of Mara, an office she held because she wore the Ring of the Patient Tongue and could interpret the oldest inscriptions.

Through those inscriptions she learned what had been done to the Warden. He had not offered himself. He had been made useful after death and denied even the freedom to understand his own imprisonment. The priestess judged that no sanctuary of Mara could preserve such a thing and still claim to guide change without destroying the self.

On a moonless night she entered the transmutation chamber, lit the undying braziers, and stood within its circle. Her intended rite was bold, compassionate, and badly phrased for the power she addressed. She meant to separate the guardian's identity from the road's function, permit the captive to die, and transfer the burden of the seal into an empty funerary vessel.

The buried work obeyed the command it understood.

It separated identity from body.

The priestess's head tore free without dying. The ring preserved comprehension. The road preserved her voice. The transmutation circle preserved awareness. None preserved her humanity. Her body remained in an upright sarcophagus while her living head grew leathery wings and circled the chamber, still attempting to speak the last command of the rite.

The attendants who found her could neither restore nor kill her. They sealed the creature beside its body. The ring fell near the sarcophagus, beyond the hand that had worn it. In time pain, hunger, and incomplete language reduced the attempted command to a shriek.

This is the origin of the winged horror in Hightower.

It was not placed there to profane a holy place. It is what remained when a holy person asked an ancient mechanism to understand mercy.

Some part of the old duty appears to survive within the creature. Its cry is an attack, but the pattern of its appearances suggests a warning as well: it rises when an unprepared traveler approaches the deeper seal. Whether the priestess still knows whom she warns cannot be established.

VII. Orik Fenstone's Unfinished Argument

Orik Fenstone came to Hightower because roads near the ruin behaved badly. He found a weathered star-mark that local memory called the Warden's sign and collected accounts of children once forbidden to play near similarly marked stones. He concluded that the tower stood upon a way-marker, a burial house, and a road-watch, each built after the other and each mistaken for the whole.

His notes distinguish the chambers with unusual economy. The eastern room was, in his judgment, too orderly to be abandoned. The western chamber was simply wrong.

There he found the headless body and the failing marks of containment. Lacking the Ring of the Patient Tongue, he could not read the oldest seal. He reinforced what he could with wax, lampblack, and his own blood. The crude additions later found upon the sarcophagus are his work, not the work of the first builders.

He also recognized a recurring phrase in the funerary text, rendered in an old rhyme as the tongue that follows the road. He suspected that it referred to a key for translation and command. He never found the ring and did not live to test the theory.

Fenstone died of spider venom before completing his concordance. The spider and the lesser creatures of the ruin belonged to the ruin's later ecology, not its design. This fact is worth stating because scholars have an incurable desire to make every rat part of a ritual.

The present volume owes him its central argument: Hightower cannot be understood by asking what one people built there. One must ask what each age found, what it added, what it misunderstood, and what continued working in spite of them all.

VIII. Counsel for Those Who Must Enter

Hightower should not be treated as a dead temple, a mere tomb, or an abandoned fort. It has been all three, but the oldest work beneath it remains capable of response.

If the lower chambers must be entered, the surviving customs recommend the following:

  1. Speak your name before crossing the oldest threshold.
  2. Keep a written account of every person who enters, departs, or dies within.
  3. Do not remove bowls, rings, pearls, tablets, or crystals merely because a later keeper placed them in a chest.
  4. If an object must be moved, record its former place and return it if you can.
  5. Do not mistake preservation for kindness. The place may keep a life, a voice, or a duty long after mercy would have let it end.
  6. If a storm gathers while the sky beyond the hill remains clear, assume that Hightower is acting before assuming that the weather is strange.
  7. If a cry rises from the western chamber, retreating may be wisdom rather than cowardice. Listen only if you possess the means to understand what the cry once was.

The last leaf bears no conclusion, only a sentence copied in three different hands:

A road is safest when it knows where it leads. A traveler is safest when the road does not decide who they must remain.

No surviving roll gives the same number of people who entered the buried complex. No two casualty lists agree. Some veterans returned to Snakemere before the first warning of the evacuation arrived. Others emerged weeks afterward with no memory of the road home. Several names appear in both pension rolls and burial ledgers.

What can be established is narrower.

March Keep Intermezzo

The Keep at the Hinge — Player Packet

This packet may be read and discussed out of character. It contains the part of the world currently open for table-level design and political play. It does not require the characters to possess every piece of institutional vocabulary used here.

Tonight has two modes:

  • Establish: The table helps decide what March Keep has already been like. The DM may accept, revise, combine, or veto suggestions to preserve hidden facts and continuity.
  • Act: The characters try to change how the keep operates. These choices meet NPC resistance, require leverage, and create consequences in play.

By the end of the intermezzo, the table should have:

  1. A usable design for March Keep.
  2. One political question the characters want to force into the open.
  3. One concrete move they intend to make.
  4. A clear target for the next prepared session.

1. Brinna's Tour: Build the Keep as We Walk

Opening

Steward Brinna Holt does not wait for the courtyard to become quiet.

She walks with a ring of keys in one hand and a wax tablet in the other, pointing without slowing: gatehouse, cistern, command hall, ration line, infirmary, south gate.

“March Keep does not possess spare space,” she says. “It possesses space someone has not required yet.”

Beyond the southern wall, scores of cooking fires mark the refugee camp. Inside, a bell rings from the signal tower. Two soldiers carry a sealed chest toward the property enclosure while a cook argues that whatever is inside the chest cannot be more useful than the grain sacks displaced to store it.

Brinna continues walking.

“I will show you the keep we have. If you see the keep we need, say so before someone dies wishing you had.”

Fixed bones

These facts are already established:

  • March Keep occupies a narrow ridge. Its interior is roughly 180 feet east–west by 145 feet north–south.
  • Its stone walls are credible defenses, reinforced by newer timber platforms.
  • It was built for roughly 80 soldiers plus support staff.
  • It currently holds about 62 regular soldiers, but only about 44 can immediately take a wall or field assignment.
  • Roughly 22 support staff and 20–25 refugee auxiliaries keep it functioning.
  • Approximately 240–260 Snakemere refugees live outside the southern wall in the South Ditch, also called the Canvas Ward.
  • If the keep is seriously attacked, there is not enough protected space for everyone.
  • The western gate is the principal public entrance. A smaller eastern postern serves riders and controlled departures. The southern gate connects the fort to the refugee camp.
  • Maron is held inside as both a prisoner and a protected witness.

Everything below is a working design. Rearrange or reinterpret it if the table finds a stronger answer.

Tour stops and design knobs

At each stop, establish at least one physical detail and one social fact. The political prompt is something the characters may later try to change.

Stop 1: Western approach and gatehouse

Required functions: wagon inspection, alarm bell, guardroom, evidence intake, access to the wall walk.

People: Biggs, the competent and dry gate guard; Wedge, his friendly partner who attempted a truth-and-liar riddle without first deciding which guard would lie.

Establish:

  • What makes this look like a frontier checkpoint rather than a city gate?
  • What improvised defense has been added since Snakemere fell?
  • What admission custom reveals whether the keep values control, hospitality, or speed?

Political pressure: Who is admitted, searched, disarmed, delayed, or turned away—and who has authority to make exceptions?

Stop 2: Central yard and cistern

Required functions: muster ground, ration line, wagon traffic, public announcements, water access.

Four boards compete for attention:

  • Missing people
  • Regional contracts
  • Ration and labor assignments
  • Orders and public notices

Establish:

  • Which board occupies the most prominent position?
  • What activity makes the yard feel overcrowded today?
  • What protected or ceremonial feature has been repurposed for emergency work?

Political pressure: Space on a public board determines which problems appear official and which remain private grief.

Stop 3: Command hall, signal tower, and holding corridor

Required functions: Maeric's office, route maps, dispatches, witnessed testimony, signal equipment, cells, and Maron's guarded room.

Establish:

  • How visible is the holding corridor from the working parts of the keep?
  • Is testimony normally heard privately, before selected witnesses, or where the yard can listen?
  • What feature makes clear that March Keep is prepared for military emergencies but not major civil trials?

Political pressure: Maeric controls custody. Brinna controls records and access. Neither possesses uncontested authority to issue a final judgment against an Idylwind charter officer.

Stop 4: Quartermaster, armory, and property enclosure

Required functions: food, tools, weapons, tents, captured equipment, refugee property, and disputed Golden Ledger cargo.

Establish:

  • What supply is visibly closest to running out?
  • How are personal property, military salvage, criminal evidence, and Ledger cargo kept distinct?
  • What item is technically evidence but obviously useful right now?

Political pressure: Every sealed sack preserves evidence. Every opened sack may feed people. “Do not touch it” is also a decision about who goes without.

Stop 5: Barracks, mess, and guest loft

Required functions: soldier bunks, communal meals, transient quarters, and two former junior-officer rooms assigned to the party.

Establish:

  • Which groups eat together, and which remain separated?
  • What has been moved, rationed, or improvised to make room?
  • What harmless argument has become strangely important because everyone is exhausted?

Political pressure: Sleeping and eating arrangements reveal whether refugee auxiliaries are treated as defenders, laborers, guests, or a population being managed.

Stop 6: Chapel-infirmary

Required functions: treatment cots, medicines, oath-taking, death records, private protection, and the Threefold shrine.

The shrine expresses three questions:

  • Aor, the Sun: What is true and lawful?
  • Mara, the Moon: What must be preserved through change?
  • Serath, the Star: Where does this choice lead?

Establish:

  • How are the three parts of the shrine physically represented?
  • What ordinary medical or clerical work happens inside each sacred space?
  • Where can a vulnerable person be protected without being treated as a prisoner?

Political pressure: Kellin can be treated as a child under sanctuary, a protected witness, a ward of an institution, or a strategic magical asset. Those descriptions grant different people authority over him.

Stop 7: Smithy, stable, and eastern postern

Required functions: repairs, wagon fittings, tools, messenger horses, scouting dispatches, and a controlled exit.

Establish:

  • What repair backlog visibly obstructs normal work?
  • How many usable mounts remain, and what are they being preserved for?
  • What can leave through the postern without attracting attention from the main yard?

Political pressure: A repaired wagon can retrieve refugees, move grain, support a patrol, or carry a delegation. A rested horse can scout a road or carry a legal demand. Priority is policy wearing work clothes.

Stop 8: South gate, Canvas Ward, and graves

Required functions: controlled camp access, labor assignments, water queues, cookfires, missing-person work, burial, and night watch.

Establish:

  • Can the refugees see into the keep, and can the garrison see into the camp?
  • Who controls movement through the southern gate?
  • What part of the camp has become civic rather than temporary despite everyone still calling it temporary?

Political pressure: The wall protects the refugees and excludes them at the same time. Their location outside it affects their safety, political standing, and ability to be heard.

2. Why Everyone Can Claim March Keep

March Keep stands where several systems of authority stop fitting together.

  • It is built on land claimed by the March Crown.
  • Its castellan is nominated by the Crown but commissioned by Idylwind's High Bench.
  • It is supplied through Idylwind's Chamber of Routes, where the Golden Ledger holds enormous influence.
  • It protects farms and roads whose food comes from King's Field.
  • Those roads cross older grazing and travel corridors claimed by the western steppe banners.
  • It now shelters survivors of Snakemere, a settlement Idylwind calls a charter holding and the refugees call their home.

No single institution can command everything March Keep needs. This is why political action is possible: each power possesses something the others cannot simply take.

3. The Maron Crisis

What is already known

  • Maron was the Golden Ledger's senior factor at Snakemere.
  • She controlled commercial wagons, stores, teamsters, guards, expedition records, and strategic cargo.
  • During the evacuation, Ledger property remained attached to wagons and guards while civilians were denied unrestricted access.
  • Some refugee groups departed late, fragmented, or without adequate protection. People died, disappeared, or were captured on the roads.
  • Some of the preserved cargo now feeds and equips survivors at March Keep.
  • Maron is unarmed and confined. She is also one of the most important surviving witnesses to the evacuation and the policies behind it.
  • March Keep can investigate the failure and maintain custody. Its power to deliver a final sentence against an Idylwind charter officer is disputed.
  • Advocate Selde Orlan and an organized Golden Ledger delegation are expected from the east. They will seek Maron's transfer and discuss grain, medicine, tools, credit, and support for the camp.

What remains genuinely unresolved

  • Which orders came from Maron and which came from officials above her?
  • How much discretion did she possess once the evacuation began?
  • What did each protected load contain?
  • Which wagons could actually have carried more people?
  • Were manifests or priority markings altered?
  • How many people survived because the cargo reached March Keep?
  • Can Maron's testimony establish institutional responsibility beyond her own choices?
  • What does justice owe the refugees besides, or instead of, a sentence against one person?

The three claims to authority

  1. March Keep: It holds Maron, shelters the survivors, protects the affected roads, and inherited the consequences of Snakemere's collapse.
  2. Idylwind: Maron acted as a charter officer over Idylwind property and contracted personnel, so only a recognized Idylwind tribunal can issue a binding judgment.
  3. The refugees: Snakemere became their home, and the institution that designed the evacuation policy cannot possess the exclusive right to judge it.

These claims can be combined, negotiated, or forced into conflict. None automatically answers whether Maron is guilty.

Political knobs the characters can turn

These are not mutually exclusive menu options. A proposal becomes stronger when it identifies who must agree, what they receive, and what happens if they refuse.

4. Other Knobs Within Reach

Refugee government

The Canvas Ward already contains households, work crews, cooks, burial workers, missing-person records, guards, and people who speak for groups of survivors. The characters can support its development into:

  • A temporary camp administered by March Keep
  • An elected refugee council
  • A provisional continuation of Snakemere's civic community
  • Several independent household and work associations

This affects who may negotiate, testify, receive property, request land, and speak for the dead or missing.

Space inside the walls

The keep cannot shelter everyone. The characters can propose priorities for protected space:

  • Wounded people
  • Children and caregivers
  • Witnesses and records
  • Refugee auxiliaries
  • Food and medicine
  • Horses and wagons
  • Prisoners and disputed property

Any priority says something about which future the keep intends to preserve.

Kellin's protection

Kellin is a child, a survivor, and the subject of unusual magical attention. The characters can influence:

  • Who may examine or question him
  • Whether the chapel, Maeric, Idylwind, or his chosen protectors hold authority
  • Whether information about him is recorded, restricted, or deliberately obscured
  • Whether he travels to the Star Shrine

Protection can become possession while everyone involved continues calling it protection.
